Asian small-clawed otter

From the Fresno Chaffee Zoo

The Asian small-clawed otter (Aonyx cinereus) is a small otter species native to parts of South and Southeast Asia. It is known for its social behavior, partially webbed feet, and small claws.

Overview

Asian small-clawed otters are members of the family Mustelidae, which also includes weasels, skunks, and ferrets.

They are the smallest otter species and are characterized by partially webbed toes and small claws that do not extend beyond their toe pads.

Physical description

Asian small-clawed otters have slender, elongated bodies covered in dense fur. Their fur is generally dark brown on the upper body and lighter underneath.

Gray or white markings may appear around the face and throat.

Diet and feeding

Asian small-clawed otters primarily feed on crustaceans and mollusks. Their diet may also include fish, insects, frogs, and small reptiles.

They use their sensitive paws to search for prey in muddy riverbeds and other shallow-water environments.

Adults generally weigh between 6 and 12 pounds. Their body length ranges from 16 to 24 inches, with tails measuring approximately 10 to 16 inches.

Behavior

Asian small-clawed otters are social animals that live in family groups. Groups may contain up to 12 individuals.

They are diurnal, meaning they are generally active during the day.

These otters are also known for their playful behavior. They may wrestle, slide down muddy banks, and interact with objects such as sticks and rocks.

Conservation status

The Asian small-clawed otter is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species.

Its population is threatened by habitat loss and degradation, pollution, hunting, and illegal wildlife trafficking.

Conservation efforts focus on protecting wetland habitats, reducing illegal trade, and supporting the survival of wild otter populations.

Habitat and range

Asian small-clawed otters inhabit freshwater environments, including rivers, streams, wetlands, and mangroves.

They are found across parts of South and Southeast Asia, including Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, and the Philippines.

They prefer areas with abundant food, shallow water, and vegetation that provides shelter.
